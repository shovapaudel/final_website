Paudel, S.
Undergraduate Thesis / Pre-print

Full Text

📖 Abstract:

Invasive alien species are among the major drivers of biodiversity decline, alongside land-use change and pollution. They pose a significant threat to biodiversity, particularly under changing climatic conditions. This study examines the distribution patterns of Parthenium hysterophorus L. in Nepal under current and future climate scenarios (medium carbon emissions (SSP245) and high carbon emissions (SSP585)) up to the year 2100, using ensemble species distribution modeling.

Species occurrence data were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), while 19 bioclimatic variables and two topographical variables were downloaded from WorldClim. Binary logistic regression was performed using five machine learning models and two statistical models, all meeting performance thresholds of area under the curve (AUC) > 0.85 and true skill statistic (TSS) > 0.6. Model outputs were ensembled using a weighted average approach based on TSS to generate final suitability maps. Among the predictors, mean diurnal range was the most influential, followed by the minimum temperature of the coldest month and precipitation of the coldest month.

Under current climatic conditions, P. hysterophorus occupies 22.54% of Nepal's total area. The highest suitability is observed in the Terai (81.15%), followed by the Siwalik (50.23%), Middle Mountain (16.75%), and High Mountain (1.15%) regions, while no suitable area is found in the High Himalaya. By years 2080–2100, under the high emission scenario, the suitable area is projected to expand to 42.39% of Nepal, compared to 37.19% under the medium emission scenario. A notable shift of species toward northern regions was observed. To conclude, climate change is likely to exacerbate the invasion of P. hysterophorus in Nepal. The invasion shift towards the northern region can pose a significant threat to abandoned agricultural lands in the Middle Mountains. Continuous monitoring and management efforts are therefore essential to prevent its further spread into new habitats.
